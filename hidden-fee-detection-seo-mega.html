The United States faces an estimated $500 billion annual hidden fee problem that affects virtually every consumer and business in the country, a staggering figure that has been thoroughly documented and verified by multiple federal agencies including the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau which reported in 2025 that junk fees alone cost consumers over $90 billion annually leading to proposed regulations targeting excessive overdraft fees, late payment penalties exceeding legally established safe harbor amounts, surprise overdraft charges on debit card transactions that were authorized with sufficient funds at the time of authorization, unnecessary mortgage closing costs for services that provide no value to borrowers, and a wide range of other hidden charges that the bureau has determined to be unfair, deceptive, or abusive practices under federal consumer protection law. The Federal Trade Commission has similarly pursued numerous enforcement actions against companies engaged in deceptive pricing practices including false reference pricing where inflated original prices make discounts appear larger than they actually are, hidden fees disclosed only in fine print rather than clearly presented to consumers before they make purchase decisions, and drip pricing where mandatory fees are added late in the purchase process after the consumer has already invested significant time in the transaction making it more difficult for them to walk away from the purchase. The problem extends across virtually every category of consumer spending from essential utilities and insurance to banking services, medical care, home improvement contracts, and entertainment subscriptions.

The root cause of this crisis is a fundamental and persistent information asymmetry between companies and consumers that has existed for decades but has worsened significantly as pricing structures have become more complex. Companies have complete knowledge of every fee they charge, every pricing term in their agreements, and the true cost of the services they provide. Consumers have no practical way to verify whether individual charges on their bills and statements are legitimate without spending hours researching industry pricing standards across multiple different providers, each of whom may use completely different terminology for identical fee types making direct comparison difficult or impossible. Telecom providers charge administrative fees of $1.99 to $3.99 per line per month that are described as covering regulatory compliance costs but actually represent pure profit for providers with no corresponding service obligation or regulatory requirement attached to these specific charges. Banks use transaction reordering practices that process transactions from largest to smallest dollar amount to maximize the number of overdraft fees that can be charged per day, generating billions in revenue from a practice that has no benefit to consumers. Medical providers use complex coding systems with over 10,000 individual CPT codes that make billing errors difficult for patients to detect without specialized knowledge.
