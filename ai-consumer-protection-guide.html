The Consumer Protection Crisis

Hidden fees and deceptive pricing practices have reached epidemic proportions in the United States, costing consumers an estimated $500 billion annually according to consumer advocacy organizations, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, and the Federal Trade Commission. The CFPB has documented that junk fees alone cost consumers over $90 billion in 2025, leading to proposed regulations targeting excessive overdraft fees, credit card late payment penalties, surprise overdraft charges on debit card transactions, unnecessary mortgage closing costs, and a wide range of other hidden charges that the bureau has determined to be unfair, deceptive, or abusive practices. The FTC has pursued numerous enforcement actions against companies engaged in deceptive pricing practices including false reference pricing where inflated original prices make discounts appear larger, hidden fees disclosed only in fine print rather than clearly presented to consumers before purchase, and drip pricing where mandatory fees are added late in the purchase process after the consumer has already committed to the transaction. These enforcement actions have recovered billions in consumer restitution, but individual consumers must have tools to identify hidden fees and enforce their rights.

Types of Consumer Protection Violations AI Detects

Junk Fees (CFPB Targeted Categories)

The CFPB's 2025 junk fee rule identified eight specific categories of fees that the bureau has determined to be unfair, deceptive, or abusive practices requiring regulatory action. These include bank overdraft fees collected through transaction reordering where the bank processes transactions from largest to smallest to maximize the number of overdraft fees charged, credit card late payment fees exceeding the CFPB safe harbor amounts of $30 for first violations and $41 for subsequent violations within six billing cycles, surprise overdraft fees on debit card transactions that were authorized with sufficient funds at the time of authorization but later caused an overdraft due to transaction processing delays, auto loan add-on products like GAP insurance and extended warranties that are marked up 100 to 300 percent above wholesale cost, and mortgage closing cost junk fees that include duplicative or entirely unnecessary services like courier fees for documents that are delivered electronically. AI detection at DetectHiddenFees.com identifies each of these fee categories in consumer financial documents and flags charges that may exceed applicable regulatory thresholds or violate consumer protection standards.

Deceptive Pricing Practices

The FTC has established clear guidelines prohibiting deceptive pricing practices under Section 5 of the FTC Act which prohibits unfair or deceptive acts or practices in commerce. AI identifies false reference pricing where an inflated regular price is displayed to make a sale price appear more attractive than it actually is. Bait-and-switch tactics where an advertised price is not actually available are also flagged. Hidden surcharges that are disclosed only in fine print rather than clearly presented to consumers before purchase are identified by comparing pricing disclosures across the full document. AI also detects drip pricing practices where mandatory fees are disclosed only at the end of the purchasing process after the consumer has already invested time in the transaction, making it more difficult to walk away.

Unfair Contract Terms

Consumer contracts often contain terms that may violate state unfair trade practices and consumer protection laws. AI identifies mandatory arbitration clauses that waive the consumer's right to sue in court and require disputes to be resolved through private arbitration, class action waivers that prevent consumers from joining together to pursue claims against companies, unconscionable penalty provisions that charge fees far exceeding any actual damages the company could sustain, and liquidated damages clauses that set penalty amounts far above reasonable estimates of actual damages. Each of these terms is flagged with references to applicable state laws.
